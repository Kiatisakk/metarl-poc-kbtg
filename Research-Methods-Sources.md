# Primary-source audit: DP, RL, and Meta-RL references

This note audits what the supplied papers demonstrate. PDF page numbers below refer to the file page index shown by a PDF viewer, which may differ from the page number printed in the paper. Findings about a K PLUS prototype are **inferences**, not claims tested by these sources.

## Das et al., *Dynamic Optimization for Multi-Goals Wealth Management*

Source: [MultWealthGoals.pdf](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf).

- **Problem and method.** The authors choose at each period which predeclared goals to fulfil (including partial versions) and which portfolio to hold. The objective is expected total utility from fulfilled goals, optionally with terminal wealth utility. Their Bellman recursion uses time and a discretized wealth grid, goal costs and utilities, and transition probabilities derived mainly from geometric Brownian motion. [PDF pp. 6–7](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=6), [12–14](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=12).
- **Assumptions and inputs.** Goal timing, costs, relative utilities, portfolio options, and planned cash infusions are supplied. The paper explicitly says that goal utilities cannot be validly assigned without investor feedback; users can iteratively revise them after seeing goal-attainment probabilities. This is preference elicitation, not a learned user model. [PDF pp. 8–9](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=8), [14–15](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=14).
- **Evaluation.** Numerical scenarios show how optimal decisions and attainment probabilities change. A large illustrative 60-year case with 301 full goals and 138 partial alternatives ran in 17 seconds using a split optimization on a desktop; examples use portfolios built from historical US fund returns. These are modeled comparisons, not a field trial of changed household behavior. [PDF p. 3](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=3), [pp. 14–16](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=14).
- **Scope limits.** The paper's fixed goal schedule does not let an unfulfilled goal move to a later date or depend on whether an earlier goal was taken. That is a limit of its stated state and goal specification. Adding goal-status or previous-action variables could encode such dependencies in DP, at additional state-space cost; it is not an inherent inability of Bellman methods. [PDF p. 5](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=5).
- **Implication for the hackathon.** A small finite-horizon cash allocation problem with explicit obligations, preference weights, and a tractable state grid has genuine DP structure. Portfolio selection, hundreds of goals, and investment-return dynamics are separate complexity, not prerequisites.

## Das et al., *A Meta Reinforcement Learning Approach to Goals-Based Wealth Management*

Source: [2605.02300v1.pdf](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf).

- **Problem and MDP.** At each yearly step, the two agents choose whether to fulfil one all-or-nothing goal and select one investment portfolio. Wealth then evolves stochastically under the chosen portfolio. The objective is expected sum of goal utilities; an auxiliary intrinsic reward steers learning toward forward-simulation indicators. The 26 policy inputs normalize time, wealth relative to future goal costs, aggregated future goal utilities and costs, and two simulated indicators. [PDF pp. 4–7](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=4), [27–33](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=27).
- **What “Meta-RL” means here.** Five dual-PPO models are trained over generated wealth-goal scenarios, then queried as **pretrained, zero-shot policies** for new scenarios. In testing, the actors receive newly computed current-state inputs and output actions; there is no reported per-user fine-tuning, recurrent history-based inference of an unknown preference, or within-user learning experiment. A policy can react to updated wealth without learning the user's hidden dynamics. [PDF pp. 3, 7–8](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=3), [27–28, 34–36](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=27).
- **Training and evaluation.** Each of five random-seed runs has 1,000 epochs with 500 simulated episodes per epoch and generated scenarios. The principal test suite is 66 hand-designed new investor problems; 10,000 simulated paths per case estimate performance. Mean RL-to-DP achieved-utility ratio is 0.978 (minimum 0.917). The experiment compares policies under its simulator and assumed utilities, not observed banking customers. [PDF pp. 8, 12](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=8), [34–36](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=34).
- **Runtime and practical caveats.** Table 1 gives mean current-action inference of 20.94 ms when a goal decision is needed and 9.277 ms for portfolio only, versus 2,198 ms for per-case DP. Estimating expected attained utility requires many policy queries and rollouts, making runtimes comparable for the simpler cases. Training used about four hours and experiments primarily ran on a 96-vCPU, 192-GB AWS instance. These figures do not establish “20 million users in under 10 ms.” [PDF pp. 8–9](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=8), [34–36](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=34).
- **Regime robustness is conditional generalization.** New test portfolios lie on externally recomputed efficient frontiers; initial wealth is rescaled for comparison. Good zero-shot transfer in those stress tests does not establish online discovery of a user's spending propensity or preferences from their transaction history. [PDF pp. 12–14](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=12).
- **DP caveat.** The paper says its DP formulation cannot handle constraints based on previous actions. This holds with only `(time, wealth)` as its state; augmenting state with a previous action can make such a problem Markovian and DP-solvable if the enlarged state remains tractable. [PDF pp. 7–8](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=7).
- **Implication for the hackathon.** Citing this paper does not justify calling a pretrained policy “adaptive” to a new first jobber. A true adaptation experiment would need an unobserved user-specific parameter, interaction history that reveals it, a belief or recurrent policy that updates its decisions, and held-out user profiles. It should beat both a pooled policy and a simple online estimator under matched simulation assumptions.

## Krohné and Pagrotsky, *Dynamic Optimization of Individual Financial Well-Being*

Source: [FULLTEXT01.pdf](ref/FULLTEXT01.pdf).

- **Problem and model.** This Swedish undergraduate thesis models people aged 18–30 allocating net cash flow among a liquid buffer, housing down payment, short-term savings, and long-term savings. State is the four balances; actions are signed allocations constrained to sum to net cash flow. Positive allocation shares use 25% increments, and deficits use a buffer-first withdrawal rule. Income, expenses, life events, and short-term spending create uncertainty. The objective aggregates weighted account/goal utilities over a finite horizon. [PDF pp. 2, 31–33, 36–40](ref/FULLTEXT01.pdf#page=31).
- **Data and evaluation.** Public Swedish data, return assumptions, literature, and aggregated bank context calibrate a stylized model; the authors state that **no individual customer data** enter it. They solve the DP and evaluate specifications with 5,000 Monte Carlo trajectories plus sensitivity analysis. This is a simulation study, not evidence of Thai first-jobber prevalence or real user uptake. [PDF pp. 21–24](ref/FULLTEXT01.pdf#page=21).
- **Findings and limits.** Under the modeled scenarios, housing weights and a threshold bonus substantially change down-payment attainment; a balanced weighting performs better under some constrained-income scenarios. When a housing target becomes unattainable, the threshold bonus can lead the model to divert allocations away from housing. Results are sensitive to weights and bonus choice. The authors caution that subjective stress, preferences, and biases are omitted, parameters are partly assumptions, and grid resolution affects performance. [PDF pp. 47–51, 52–56](ref/FULLTEXT01.pdf#page=47).
- **Implication for the hackathon.** The thesis is direct support for *testing a small sequential allocation model* and for reporting utility sensitivity. Its four-account, multi-year system is already beyond the smallest K PLUS decision problem. It provides no evidence that RL, much less Meta-RL, is needed.

## Deaton, *Saving and Liquidity Constraints*

Source: [Deaton_1991_Saving_and_Liquidity_Constraints_w3196.pdf](ref/Deaton_1991_Saving_and_Liquidity_Constraints_w3196.pdf).

- This is a theoretical consumption-saving analysis under borrowing constraints and uncertain labor income. Under relatively impatient preferences and iid income, assets can act as a buffer against bad income draws; as income persistence changes, the optimal buffer behavior changes, and the abstract explicitly notes cases with no saving. It supports modeling a shortfall or reserve trade-off, **not** a universal reserve threshold or a tested app intervention. [PDF pp. 2–4](ref/Deaton_1991_Saving_and_Liquidity_Constraints_w3196.pdf#page=2).

## Cross-paper cautions for method selection

1. **Sequential does not automatically mean RL.** When transitions and rewards are explicit and the state is small, finite-horizon DP gives an inspectable optimal policy and a benchmark. The cited RL paper gains most clearly from reusing a pretrained policy across many large, varied investor problems. [MultWealthGoals.pdf pp. 13–14](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=13), [2605.02300v1.pdf pp. 7–9](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=7).
2. **Personalization is not adaptation.** Explicitly supplying goal utility weights personalizes a DP solution; a pretrained network conditioning on stated parameters personalizes its output. Neither shows online inference of hidden individual preferences. That question requires its own identifiability design and experiment. [MultWealthGoals.pdf pp. 8–9](ref/MultWealthGoals.pdf#page=8), [2605.02300v1.pdf pp. 34–36](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=34).
3. **A simulated win is conditional on the simulator.** If the same transition model and reward weights define both training and evaluation, better simulated utility does not by itself establish better financial behavior for K PLUS users. Vary demand, income, and preference models, and report constraint violations and sensitivity alongside average utility. [FULLTEXT01.pdf pp. 21–24](ref/FULLTEXT01.pdf#page=21), [pp. 47–49](ref/FULLTEXT01.pdf#page=47), [2605.02300v1.pdf pp. 8, 12](ref/2605.02300v1.pdf#page=8).
